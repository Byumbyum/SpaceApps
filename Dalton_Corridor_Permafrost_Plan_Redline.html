Ground motion on the Dalton corridor: a NISAR investigation of thaw, people and potential risk

Historical radar studies document seasonal ground motion and post-fire subsidence in Arctic Alaska. We will test three contrasting landscapes—relatively undisturbed tundra, the Anaktuvuk burn area, and a developed highway/oilfield corridor—against newer NISAR observations. NISAR’s L-band may preserve coherence in some vegetated areas; we will measure that advantage rather than assume it.

Study area: one corridor, three sites

The Dalton Highway begins north of Fairbanks near Livengood and runs north to Deadhorse; the Trans-Alaska Pipeline broadly parallels much of the route but not every road segment. The larger corridor traverses forest, the Brooks Range and North Slope tundra. Our northern study box is only one portion of that corridor.

Initial northern study window: approximately 68.5°N–70.5°N, 152°W–147°W, subject to confirmed overlapping product footprints. Do not label this the full Dalton corridor. Use separate southern windows for the Brooks Range debris lobes and Fairbanks-area forest comparison, with each map showing its true extent and dates.

InSAR and NISAR: what each one is

InSAR is a technique. It compares radar phase from repeat acquisitions with similar viewing geometry. Motion along the radar line of sight contributes to phase change, but the interferogram also contains non-deformation terms:

Δφdef=4πλΔrMagnitude relation after non-deformation corrections; the sign depends on the LOS and phase conventions. The 4π factor reflects the two-way path.

NISAR's L-band wavelength is about 24 cm, so one complete interferometric phase cycle represents about 12 cm of line-of-sight range change (not automatically vertical sinking):

Δrfringe=λ2≈12cm

The measured phase may contain DEM/topography residuals, tropospheric and ionospheric delays, orbital artifacts, surface changes and unwrapping errors. GUNW includes ionospheric phase estimates and geocoded lookup tables for some external corrections; these are not removed by default. Inspect metadata, apply the appropriate corrections with the documented signs and units, and verify residuals. For approximately vertical motion with negligible horizontal movement, LOS displacement can be converted using the local incidence angle:

dvert≈dLOScosθOnly under negligible horizontal motion and appropriate incidence-angle/sign conventions; do not use for downslope movement without an additional motion model.

NISAR is a satellite with two kinds of product we use:

  • NISAR · InSAR GUNW: geocoded unwrapped interferograms, nominal 12-day nearest-neighbor pairs, 80 m posting for unwrapped phase, with coherence. After corrections and referencing, this estimates relative line-of-sight displacement; it does not directly classify thaw.
  • NISAR · backscatter GCOV: calibrated, geocoded radar backscatter in available polarizations (not InSAR). Changes may reflect wetness, vegetation, roughness, seasonal thaw/freeze or acquisition differences; do not label backscatter alone as thaw.

Other satellites also give InSAR: Sentinel-1 · InSAR (C-band, 2016 onwards as OPERA DISP-S1) and ALOS · InSAR (L-band, 2006–2010).

Rules that keep InSAR honest

  • Coherence (0–1) is one interferometric quality indicator, not an overall probability of truth. Apply a stated threshold, inspect connected-component/unwrapping masks and test whether L-band coherence is higher in the chosen vegetated scene.
  • Snow, freeze–thaw and moisture changes can compromise pairs; prioritize verified snow-free dates (often summer, but confirm per acquisition), and retain observation-date metadata.
  • It is relative. Every value is measured against a reference point and date. Do not assume a gravel pad is stable: assess reference stability and disclose residual regional offsets and reference uncertainty.
  • Combining pairs into a time series can accumulate unwrapping, atmosphere, reference and surface-scattering biases. Report uncertainty and compare with independent Sentinel-1, ICESat-2 or field observations where spatial/temporal overlap permits.
2026 Arctic-specific check: NISAR release notes flag residual ionospheric effects, challenging phase unwrapping and a GUNW squint-compensation phase artifact. Inspect each selected product and its release/CRID before treating a localized anomaly as motion; compare corrected and uncorrected maps.

Filters: ruling out false signals

A pixel getting brighter or moving downward is not proof of permafrost thaw. Masks and contextual overlays reduce ambiguity; they do not conclusively rule out every alternative mechanism. Apply measurement-quality filters first, then combine contextual evidence into a separate thaw-interpretation score.

If corrected NISAR backscatter and InSAR exhibit a consistent spatiotemporal change, it strengthens a thaw hypothesis. However, the measurements are not independent ground truth: both can respond to precipitation, vegetation or changing scatterers.

Two scores, not a probability of thaw

Separate (1) whether the satellite displacement is trustworthy from (2) whether permafrost thaw is a plausible explanation. Neither 0–1 score is a calibrated probability. A high-quality movement observation can have low thaw-interpretation support, and vice versa.

1. Measurement validity: hard exclusions

Grey out invalid pixels, labeling why: water / absent valid sample mask, snow or freeze–thaw contamination on pair dates, missing or disconnected unwrapped phase, severe layover/geometry, and insufficient observations. A low-coherence pixel can be excluded under a clearly stated cutoff. Outside-permafrost ground is not an invalid radar measurement; it is excluded only from the permafrost-specific interpretation view.

2. Quality factors (Q) and interpretation factors (E)

For each score, normalize and combine applicable factors with a weighted geometric mean, publishing the factors and weights:

S=∏ifiwi,∑iwi=1Compute separately as Q and E. If any positive-weight factor is exactly zero, S is zero. An unavailable factor is 'not assessed' (with normalized weights on observed factors), not automatically zero; disclose missing evidence.

Team-defined bands: high ≥ 0.75, medium 0.50–<0.75, low <0.50. These are screening categories, not probabilities or validated performance thresholds. Test sensitivity to factor weights and thresholds; report uncertainty separately.

Calibration check: With a few known positive and negative examples, test how the indexes rank sites. Avoid saying “82% confidence” or “82% probability of thaw”; instead say “Q=0.82 (team-defined quality index).”

Metrics: four numbers carry the analysis

Long-term rate and thaw sensitivity

Where enough consistent dates exist, fit a corrected, referenced displacement time series using a trend and a seasonal thermal term; a single summer of NISAR alone cannot establish a long-term rate:

d(t)=v(t−t0)+AADDTy(t)+c+ε(t)
  • v: estimated long-term residual ground-motion rate (cm/yr); persistent subsidence may be consistent with ground-ice loss but is not automatic proof of its cause
  • A: empirical seasonal thermal sensitivity of displacement; not a direct estimate of thaw depth without physical and site calibration
  • c: a constant reference offset; ε(t) is a residual whose time correlation and uncertainty must be assessed
ADDT(t)=∑daysmax(Tair,0)Accumulated degree-days of thaw (ADDT): daily positive 2 m air temperatures summed since seasonal onset; 5 days at +10 °C = 50 °C·days. The Stefan square-root relationship motivates a candidate model for thaw depth; displacement additionally depends on ice/water content and soil properties. ERA5 is a coarse air-temperature proxy, not in situ soil temperature.

Research backing: Stefan-type thaw-depth laws motivate h≈NADDT. ReSALT-style active-layer inversion additionally requires soil/ground-ice assumptions and validation: the fitted displacement coefficient A is not simply the physical thaw-depth coefficient N. The 2024 Dalton-area study found a relatively weak ADDT–measured ALT relationship (R²≈0.42); do not claim a universal heat-to-subsidence law or an unvalidated depth accuracy.

Uneven-sinking risk

Differential settlement can stress structures, but absolute settlement, slope, design and local ground conditions also matter. For an initial screening map, estimate a spatial settlement gradient only where resolution and uncertainty support it:

β=ΔsLΔs is the measured displacement difference and L is their spacing, along a verified road/pipeline geometry. Native 80 m GUNW posting cannot resolve every road-width gradient. Propagate uncertainty in Δs; use consistently sized along-corridor windows and call categories relative monitoring priority, not engineering “critical.”

Infrastructure exposure

Overlay measurement-quality-screened, uncertainty-qualified deformation zones with independently checked road/pipeline traces and building footprints. Count km and buildings near candidate anomalies, clearly distinguishing valid observed overlap from unmapped or low-quality areas. Report per-year exposure only when comparable time-series coverage exists; these are monitoring priorities, not verified asset damage or safety ratings.

Two different yearly measurements

  • Seasonal amplitude (within-season movement). An increase is consistent with deeper thaw in some settings, but can also reflect changing soil moisture, ground ice or acquisition timing.
  • Net drift (residual change between comparable seasonal endpoints). Persistent subsidence may indicate degradation but requires excluding other processes and reference drift. Even without inferred ice loss, repeated differential settlement can matter for infrastructure.

Two horizons: act locally now, monitor globally

Short term: infrastructure, safety, health

NISAR can contribute observations of ground movement near infrastructure where coherent pixels and valid geometry exist. The β gradient and exposure tally flag locations worth inspection; they are not calibrated damage predictions, and a coarse pixel cannot establish movement directly under a narrow road or pipeline. Ground works, excavation, fill compaction or fluid extraction may also contribute, so describe candidate movement alongside infrastructure and its alternative causes.

  • Nearly 70% of current infrastructure in the permafrost domain is in areas with high potential for near-surface thaw by 2050 (Hjort et al., 2022).
  • Permafrost thaw damage to Alaska buildings and roads is estimated at $37B (SSP2-4.5) to $51B (SSP5-8.5) by mid-century.
  • 43 frozen debris lobes have been identified in the Dalton corridor through the southern Brooks Range, 23 of them less than a mile uphill of the highway.
  • In Utqiaġvik, thaw subsidence undermines traditional ice cellars. The Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium links this to foodborne illness risk and food security. Soil conditions and development also play a role.

Long term: the carbon feedback

Say "self-reinforcing feedback, with possible localized abrupt thaw", not "runaway". A subsidence map cannot identify an emissions rate or prove a climatic tipping point. Use the literature to distinguish gradual permafrost-carbon feedback from specific proposed abrupt-thaw tipping processes.

  • Permafrost holds roughly twice as much carbon as the atmosphere.
  • There is high confidence thaw will release carbon, and some regions are already net sources. The losses are irreversible on century timescales.
  • IPCC AR6's estimate of 3–41 GtC per °C of warming excludes abrupt thaw and fire-permafrost interactions, so it likely underestimates emissions.
  • Our link: InSAR and optical/DEM changes may locate candidate abrupt-subsidence and fire-affected areas relevant to permafrost-carbon discussions; verifying abrupt thaw requires morphology/temporal evidence. NISAR does not directly measure soil carbon or emissions.

What not to claim

  • Do not say “NISAR directly measures thaw” or “proves climate change.” Say it estimates ground motion; thermal, landcover and field evidence may support a permafrost interpretation. Historical climate attribution relies on broader published science.
  • One snow-free NISAR season may sample only part of the seasonal cycle, depending on valid acquisitions; do not promise a trend from NISAR 2026 alone. Historical rates can be estimated from suitable multi-year Sentinel-1 or ALOS series when available and cross-calibrated.
  • Published research suggests some undisturbed tundra can show limited residual change, and the Anaktuvuk fire area has documented post-fire subsidence. Do not predetermine which of our three sites moves most: test them and show null results if that is what the data support.
  • Radar backscatter can brighten or darken with thaw, rain, vegetation, roughness and viewing geometry; a single direction of brightness change is not a universal thaw signature. Compare compatible polarizations/modes and corroborate with other sensors.
  • Do not invent cost figures. Quote published estimates as context. Likewise never convert an unvalidated priority score into failure probability, repair-cost prediction or risk to a named community.

App structure

  • Story mode is the landing page for policymakers and the public: one visual per chapter, with plain captions.
  • Explore mode: draw an area to get the rate map, three-site trend, risk ribbon and separate Q/E evidence layers, with selectable comparison area; show insufficient coverage rather than extrapolating across unobserved regions.
  • Use the globe only as an animated intro, then a 2D map (MapLibre or Leaflet) with a polygon tool.
  • Pre-compute v, A, yearly brightness and β as map tiles. Ingest GUNW/GCOV with geospatial preprocessing, preserve masks/version/units and serve provenance with each tile; don’t portray unprocessed RRSD or QA reports as deformation maps.
  • Rename "NISAR spectrum" to "Radar layer": backscatter proxy, cross-polarized scattering (where available), and inferred ground motion. Avoid calling HV a direct vegetation-structure measurement without validation.
  • Default to quality-screened snow-free ground-motion analysis; show other seasons separately only if the data are interpretable, with a warning about freeze–thaw and temporal gaps.
